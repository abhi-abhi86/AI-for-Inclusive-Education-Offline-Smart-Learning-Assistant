The hydrological cycle, commonly known as the water cycle, describes the continuous movement of water on, above, and below the surface of the Earth. Driven primarily by solar thermal energy and gravity, water continually shifts between liquid, solid, and vapor states across different natural reservoirs.

The cycle initiates when solar heat causes liquid water from oceans, lakes, and rivers to evaporate into airborne vapor. Concurrently, terrestrial vegetation releases water vapor through a biological transpiration process via leaf stomata. As the warm, moist air ascends into lower-temperature regions of the troposphere, it cools, causing vapor molecules to condense around microscopic aerosols and form clouds or fog.

When condensed water droplets agglomerate and become too heavy to remain suspended against updrafts, precipitation occurs in the form of rain, snow, sleet, or hail. Upon returning to the ground, precipitation follows multiple geological paths: a portion is absorbed into soil layers through infiltration to replenish subterranean groundwater aquifers, while surface runoff flows across watersheds into freshwater streams and eventually discharges back into the global ocean.
